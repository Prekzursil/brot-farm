Chapter 1

The mathematics of escape

Everything below is one sentence, stretched out: iterate a rule, watch where the points go, count the visits. But each clause hides something worth slowing down for.

1 · One step of the machine: squaring a complex number

Write z = x + iy in polar form as r·e^{iθ} — a radius r and an angle θ. Squaring acts beautifully: z² has radius r² and angle 2θ. In Cartesian components (that’s what the code computes):

z² = (x + iy)² = (x² − y²) + i·(2xy)

So one iteration z ↦ z² + c doubles the point’s angle around the origin, squares its distance, then nudges it by the fixed constant c. Doubling angles is where all the chaos lives: angles near each other separate twice as fast each step — after k steps, a gap of ε has grown to ε·2^k. That exponential sensitivity is the fingerprint of a chaotic map, and the reason orbits trace filigree instead of blobs.

2 · Prisoners and escapees

Fix a constant c and iterate from z₀ = 0. Two futures exist. If the orbit ever crosses |z| > 2, it diverges to infinity, and provably so: past radius 2 the squaring outruns the additive nudge (for |z| ≥ max(|c|, 2), each step at least squares the excess). Those are the escapees — the raw material of every image here. Points whose orbits stay bounded forever are prisoners; the set of all c-values with prisoner orbits is the Mandelbrot set. The Antibuddhabrot, perversely, photographs the prisoners instead.

Try it: click anywhere below to choose c and watch its orbit. Land inside the cardioid for a locked-in prisoner; graze the boundary for long dramatic escapes; step outside for a quick death.

3 · The photograph: orbit density as a measure

A single orbit is a polyline; a million of them make a cloud. Formally, each escaping orbit contributes counting measure along its path; the Buddhabrot is the expected density of that cloud over uniformly sampled c — estimated by Monte Carlo. The estimator’s noise obeys the photography law: per-pixel standard deviation ∝ √visits, so relative grain falls as 1/√N. The classic 74M-sample render averages ~1000 visits per lit pixel; the sparse power brots started near ~20 and were topped up until structure stood out of the grain.

Visits are also brutally heavy-tailed: the bulb’s rim collects millions while the far field counts dozens. A linear display would show one white dot. Hence the darkroom work in §6.

4 · Three channels = three depths of time

Iteration count is the hidden third dimension. An orbit that escapes in 30 steps never wanders far from the set; one that survives 1500 steps before breaking out skims the boundary for a long, long time. Rendering the same scene at three caps — 80, 400, 1500 — and assigning them to B, G, R turns depth-of-time into color: shallow fast escapes glow blue-cyan, mid-depth green-gold, and the deep survivors burn orange-red as halos. The Nebulabrot pushes the same idea to 50 / 500 / 5000 for a purely gaseous look.

5 · Escape laws are per-map theorems

“Past radius 2 you die” is true for quadratics but is not a universal law. Each update rule needs its own escape criterion, and getting it wrong quietly poisons the classifier (see the NaN bug). The rules used in this project:

FamilyRuleEscape testWhy it’s valid
zn + cx′=Re(zⁿ)+cx|z| > 2 polynomial growth dominates the additive c once |z| exceeds max(2, |c|)
sin z + c, cos z + ccosh/sinh split|z| > 10³ cosh y ~ e^|y|/2: once |y| ≳ 8 growth is exponential per step
cos-mutationsx′ = cos(x²) − y + cx|y| > 100 cos folds huge |x| back to [−1,1] in ONE step — x is a trapdoor, y is the only exit; once |y| is large, x′ ≈ −y and |y′| = |2xy| compounds
y-cosine mutationy′ = cos 2xy + cy|x| > 100 the mirror image: y′ is forever bounded in [−1, 1], so x — whose update still squares — is the only exit

6 · The darkroom: log compression between anchors

Raw counts span five orders of magnitude. The tone pipeline is:

v   = log(1 + h)                       # compress five decades into ~11 stops
lo  = percentile(v[lit], 30)          # black anchor: the 30th pct of LIT pixels
hi  = percentile(v[lit], 99.9)        # white anchor: the hot core
img = clip((v − lo) / (hi − lo)) ** γ # gamma shapes the midtones

Why percentiles and not min/max? One over-hot pixel (the bulb rim gets millions of visits) would pin the white point and starve everything else; anchors taken from the distribution of lit pixels self-calibrate to whatever the map produced. A black anchor matters just as much: the project’s first attempt normalized only the top and rendered fog — 59% mean luminance — until the 30th-percentile floor put the darkness back.

7 · Symmetry is a theorem you can measure

If a map’s coefficients are real, it commutes with conjugation: F(z̄; c̄) = conj(F(z; c)). Mirror the start, and every step mirrors — so a uniformly sampled symmetric window must produce a mirror-symmetric histogram, up to noise. Break the law on purpose — fold |2xy| (Burning Ship, Buffalo), conjugate before squaring (Tricorn — which instead gains 3-fold rotation), or add a lone −y (our mutations) — and the figure tips over. The renderer’s QC measures exactly this:

The power maps z³/z⁴/z⁵ are equivariant too — their measured correlations dip (0.5–0.76) purely because their histograms are sparse, and shot noise decorrelates faint halves. The theorem holds; the film is grainy.
